Lipid Panel Analysis Summary

The participants were limited to adults (aged 18 or greater) and BMI of under 50. 

Triglyceride:
- Right skewed data > natural log transformed for normal distribution
- BMI vs Triglyceride correlation: 0.289
- BMI vs Triglyceride correlation:
	Female: 0.283
	Male: 0.321

Total Cholesterol
- Normally distributed with slight right skew but good enough for regression
- BMI vs Total Cholesterol correlation: 0.0573
	Female: 0.022
	Male: 0.085

LDL
- Normally distributed with slight right skew
- BMI vs LDL correlation: 0.133
	Female: 0.116
	Male: 0.154

HDL
- Right skewed data > natural log transformed for normality
- BMI vs HDL correlation: -0.295
	Female: -0.330
	Male: -0.340


The lipid panel seems to be a little more telling than the urine labs. 

The two lipids that seems to show some correlation are the triglycerides and HDL. Triglyceride is positively correlated (0.289), especially in the male cohort (0.321). The HDL has a negative correlation (-0.295) and both genders seem to be affected about the same (-0.330 in females and -0.340 in males). 

The total cholesterol is combining all of the cholesterols into one value. The decision was made to exclude the total cholesterol level in the regression model, since it is a bit duplicative. 

The regression model shows that the lipids separately affect the BMI 11%, and the adjusted R squared is about the same as R-squared, meaning the model is not overfitted. The F-statistic shows that the log(triglyceride), LDL, and log(HDL) are statistically significant in predicting BMI. Triglycerides and HDL have clear relationship with BMI, which physiologically makes sense. 

Lipid panel seems to show biologically significant correlation with BMI. However, statistically, only about 11% of the BMI is explained with the lipid panel.
